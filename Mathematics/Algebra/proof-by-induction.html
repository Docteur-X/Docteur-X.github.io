Proof by Induction

Method

A statement is basically a sentence that is either true or false. It might be a sentence, or an equation or a theorem or a fact, but it must be something that can be tested as true or false.

Some examples of statements are: "My house is blue", "It is raining right now", "I have 10 dollars in my pocket". They are objectively either true or false. "3 plus 2 is 5" is a true statement, while "3 plus 2 is 7" is a false statement.

Here are some statements that depend on a variable \(n\). "\(n\) is an even number" depends on what \(n\) is. This statement is true when \(n=2\) or \(n=4\) or \( n=6 \), and false when \(n=1\) or \(n=3\) or \(n=5\).

In this website, one starts with a statement \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \). Maybe we do a maths check and found out that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \) is true at \(n=\textcolor{blue}{1},\ldots\)

There's a slick way to do this. First check that \(\textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{blue}{1})\) is true.

We then do a separate calculation. Use a placeholder letter \( k \) that can stand for any positive whole number. We then hope to show that if \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k) \) is true, then \(\textcolor{purple}{P}(k+1)\) is true.

So, if \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k=1) \) is true (which it is), then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k+1=2) \) is true. If \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k=2) \) is true (which is also true because of the previous step), then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(3) \) is true, and so on.

Examples to Convince You that it Works

Someone made a bold claim. For any number n, they say that the below statement is correct.

\[ \sum_{r=1}^n r^3 = \frac{1}{4}n^2(n+1)^2 \]

(In a clickable dropdown for the sum) \[ \sum_{r=1}^n r^3 \] means \[ 1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 + \dots + n^3 \].

We don't know if the equation is correct or not for different values of \(n\). I'll use \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \) as a shorthand to refer to whether the above equation is true or false.

Is the statement correct for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \)? If you calculate the left-hand side and the right-hand side for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \), you'll get that the formula works.

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{n}} r^3 = \sum_{r=1}^\textcolor{blue}{1} r^3 = 1^3 = 1 \)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{blue}{n}^2(\textcolor{blue}{n}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{blue}{1}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{blue}{1}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 \cdot 2^2 = 1 \)

Is it correct for \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \)? If you calculate the left-hand side and the right-hand side for \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \), you'll get that both are equal to 9.

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 = \sum_{r=2}^{\textcolor{orange}{1}} r^3 = 1^3 + 2^3 = 1 + 8 = 9 \)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{orange}{n}^2(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{orange}{2}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 9 \)

We can also check that the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \), and \( \textcolor{red}{n=4} \). We might enlist some helpers to tediously check if the statement is always true, but induction gives a smarter route.

A Way to Calculate the Correctness of \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \) for Every Whole Number \(n\)

I'm going to let \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \) in the below discussion, until I say I'm changing it to \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \) later. The orange and green color doesn't change the fact that the same idea is being used.

We know that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{orange}{n}) \) is true, i.e. the statement \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 = \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{orange}{n}^2(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \) is true.

Knowing \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{orange}{n}) \) is true, is \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{green}{n+1}) \) true as well?

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{green}{n+1}} r^3 &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 + (\textcolor{green}{n+1})^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{orange}{n}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 + (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{orange}{n}^2 + 4(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{orange}{n}^2 + 4 \cdot \textcolor{orange}{n}+4 ) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+2)^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot ( (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1) + 1 )^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{n+1})^2 \cdot (\textcolor{green}{n+1} + 1)^2 \\ \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{green}{3}) \) is true, i.e. the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{green}{n+1=3} \). We've used the fact that the statement is true for \(n\), then shown it is true for \(n+1\).

(If you'd like to see the algebra in numbers, click on this collapsible!)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{LHS} = \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{green}{3}} r^3 &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{2}} r^3 + \textcolor{green}{3}^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{orange}{2}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 + (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{orange}{2}^2 + 4(\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{orange}{2}^2 + 4 \cdot \textcolor{orange}{2}+4 ) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+2)^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1)^2 \cdot ( (\textcolor{orange}{2}+1) + 1 )^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{green}{3}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3} + 1)^2 \\ &= \text{RHS} \\ \end{aligned} \]

To recap, if we know that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{brown}{n}) \) is true, then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{brown}{n+1}) \) is true. This is the key step in induction.

We now know that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{green}{3}) \) is true. What if you start from the beginning of this subsection, but instead of using \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \), we use \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \)? The same reasoning works again.

Is the statement correct for \( \textcolor{red}{n=4} \)?

\[ \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{red}{4}} r^3 \overset{?}{=} \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{red}{4}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{red}{4}+1)^2 \]

LHS: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{red}{4}} r^3 &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{green}{3}} r^3 + \textcolor{red}{4}^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{green}{3}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^2 + (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{green}{3}^2 + 4(\textcolor{green}{3}+1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{green}{3}^2 + 4 \cdot \textcolor{green}{3}+4 ) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^2 \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+2)^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{3}+1)^2 \cdot ( (\textcolor{green}{3}+1) + 1 )^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{red}{4}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{red}{4} + 1)^2 \\ &= \text{RHS} \\ \end{aligned} \]

Thus the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{red}{n=4} \).

Hopefully you see the trick here. You know that the statement is correct for \( n = \textcolor{blue}{1},\textcolor{orange}{2},\textcolor{green}{3},\textcolor{red}{4} \). Then do the following general step: if it is true for any \(n\), it is true for \(n+1\). This is enough to prove it for all positive integers.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{purple}{n+1}} r^3 &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{brown}{n}} r^3 + (\textcolor{purple}{n+1})^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \textcolor{brown}{n}^2(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)^2 + (\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} (\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)^2 ( \textcolor{brown}{n}^2 + 4(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} (\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)^2 ( \textcolor{brown}{n}^2 + 4\textcolor{brown}{n}+4) \\ &= \frac{1}{4}(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)^2(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)^2\\ &= \frac{1}{4}(\textcolor{purple}{n+1})^2(\textcolor{purple}{n+1} + 1)^2 \end{aligned} \]

If the statement is correct for some \( \textcolor{brown}{n} \), the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{purple}{n+1} \). Try substituting \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \) or \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \) to see it in action.

So, because the statement is correct for \(n=4\), it is correct for \(n=5\), and correct for \(n=6\), and correct for \(n=7\), and so on.

So the statement is correct for every positive integer.

To recap, we need to check that the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\). Then, we use algebra to show that if the statement is true at \(n\), it must also be true at \(n+1\). That is the heart of induction.

Another Summation Example

Someone else made another bold claim. They claim that the following statement is true for every positive integer n:

\[ \sum_{r=1}^n r(r+1) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3} \]

Let's apply the procedure we've seen in the previous example.

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{n}} r(r+1) = \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{1}} r(r+1) = \textcolor{blue}{1} \cdot (\textcolor{blue}{1} + 1) = 2\)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{\textcolor{blue}{n}(\textcolor{blue}{n}+1)(\textcolor{blue}{n}+2)}{3} = \frac{\textcolor{blue}{1}(\textcolor{blue}{1}+1)(\textcolor{blue}{1}+2)}{3} = \frac{1\cdot 2\cdot 3}{3} = 2\)

Thus the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\).

Step 2: Assume that \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^n r(r+1) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3} \) for some n. We know it is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\); it might be true for other values of n too.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{purple}{n+1}} r(r+1) &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{brown}{n}} r(r+1) + (\textcolor{purple}{n+1})((\textcolor{purple}{n+1})+1) \\ &= \frac{\textcolor{brown}{n}(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)}{3} + (\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2) \\ &= \frac{(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)}{3} \cdot (\textcolor{brown}{n} + 3) \\ &= \frac{(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+3)}{3} \\ &= \frac{(\textcolor{purple}{n+1})(\textcolor{purple}{n+1}+1)(\textcolor{purple}{n+1}+2)}{3} \\ \end{aligned} \]

Thus, the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \), and is true for \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \), and true for \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \), and \( \textcolor{red}{n=4} \), and so on.

Proof of a statement related to Divisibility using Induction

Another person made another bold claim:

"The value of \( \displaystyle 3^{2n} + 11 \) calculated at any (positive integer) \(n\) is a whole number that is divisible by 4."

(Dropdown: If you have learned modular arithmetic, there is an easy proof.) \[ 3^{2n} + 11 \pmod{4} = (-1)^{2n} + 11 - 12 \pmod{4} = 1 + 11 - 12 \pmod{4} = 0 \pmod{4} \]

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\).

\[ \displaystyle 3^{2\textcolor{blue}{n}} + 11 = 3^{2 \textcolor{blue}{1}} + 11 = 3^2 + 11 = 9 + 11 = 20 = 4 \cdot 5\]

Thus, \( \displaystyle 3^{2n} + 11\) is divisible by 4 when \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\). So the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\)

Step 2. Show that if the statement is true at \(n\) (i.e. \( P(n) \) is true), then the statement is true at \( n+1 \) (i.e. \( P(n+1) \) is true).

\[ \begin{align} 3^{2(n+1)} + 11 &= 3^{2n+2} + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 3^2 + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 9 + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot (8 + 1) + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 8 + 3^{2n} + 11 \\ &= \underbrace{ (3^{2n} \cdot 2) \cdot 4 }_\text{Multiple of 4} + \underbrace{(3^{2n} + 11)}_\text{Multiple of 4} \\ &= \text{Multiple of 4} \end{align} \]

Thus, if \( \displaystyle P(n) = " 3^{2n} + 11 \text{ is divisible by 4} " \) is a true statement, then \( \displaystyle P(n+1) = " 3^{2(n+1)} + 11 \text{ is divisible by 4} " \) is a true statement too.

Then you can argue that because \( P(1) \) is true, then \( P(2) \) is true. If \( P(2) \) is true, then \( P(3) \) is true. If \( P(3) \) is true, then \( P(4) \) is true. And so on.

Proof of a Statement related to Matrices using Induction

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix}^n = \begin{pmatrix} 2n+1 & -4n \\ n & 1-2n \\ \end{pmatrix} \] is true for all positive integers n.

\( \displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix}^n = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} \dots \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix}}_{n\text{ copies}} \)

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1}\).

LHS = \( \displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} ^ \textcolor{blue}{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} \)

RHS = \( \displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 2 \textcolor{blue}{n} + 1 & - 4 \textcolor{blue}{n} \\ \textcolor{blue}{n} & 1-2 \textcolor{blue}{n} \\ \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \cdot 1 + 1 & -4 \cdot 1 \\ 1 & 1 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \)

Step 2. Show that if \( P(n) \) is true, then \( P(n+1) \) is true.

\[ \begin{align} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix}^{n+1} &= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix}^{n} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2n+1 & -4n \\ n & 1-2n \\ \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \\ \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} (2n+1) \cdot 3 + (-4n) \cdot 1 & (2n+1) \cdot -4 + (-4n) \cdot -1 \\ n \cdot 3 + (1-2n) \cdot 1 & n \cdot -4 + (1-2n) \cdot -1 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 6n + 3 - 4n & -8n - 4 + 4n \\ 3n + 1 - 2n & -4n - 1 + 2n \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2n+3 & -4n - 4 \\ n + 1 & - 2n - 1 \end{pmatrix} \\ \end{align} \]

It's relatively straightforward to find \( P(n+1) \) so let's do that instead of continuing with factoring the algebra above to get the right form, which isn't super obvious. \( P(n+1) \) is indeed the expected expression, so the whole argument closes.

Thus, we see that if we assume \( P(n) \) is true, then \( P(n+1) \) is true! Hence, by the same explanation as before (which fancy mathematicians call the "principle of induction"), the statement is true for all positive integers.
