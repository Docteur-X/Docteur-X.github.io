Proof by Induction

Method

A statement is basically a sentence that is either true or false. It might be a sentence, or an equation or a theorem or a fact, but it must be something that is definitely one or the other.

Some examples of statements are: "My house is blue", "It is raining right now", "I have 10 dollars in my pocket". They are objectively either true or false. "3 plus 2 is 5" is a true statement.

Here are some statements that depend on a variable \(n\). "\(n\) is an even number" depends on what \(n\) is. This statement is true when \(n=2\) or \(n=4\) or \( n=6 \), and false when \(n\) is odd.

In this website, one starts with a statement \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \). Maybe we do a maths check and found out that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \) is true at \(n=\textcolor{blue}{1},\textcolor{orange}{2},\textcolor{green}{3},\dots\).

There's a slick way to do this. First check that \(\textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{blue}{1})\) is true.

We then do a separate calculation. Use a placeholder letter \( k \) that can stand for any positive whole number. We then hope to show that if \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k) \) is true, then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k+1) \) is also true.

So, if \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k=1) \) is true (which it is), then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k+1=2) \) is true. If \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k=2) \) is true (which is also true because of the previous step), then \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(k+1=3) \) is true, and so on.

Examples to Convince You that it Works

Someone made a bold claim. For any number \(n\), they say that the below statement is correct.

\[ \sum_{r=1}^n r^3 = \frac{1}{4}n^2(n+1)^2 \]

(In a clickable dropdown for the sum) \[ \sum_{r=1}^n r^3 \] means \[ 1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 + \dots + n^3 \].

We don't know if the equation is correct for different values of \(n\). I'll use \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(n) \) as shorthand for whether the above equation is true or false.

Is the statement correct for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \)?

If you calculate the left-hand side and the right-hand side for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \), you'll get:

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{n}} r^3 = \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{1}} r^3 = 1^3 = 1 \)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{blue}{n}^2(\textcolor{blue}{n}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{blue}{1}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{blue}{1}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 \cdot 2^2 = 1 \)

Is it correct for \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \)?

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 = 1^3 + 2^3 = 9 \)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{orange}{n}^2(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 2^2 \cdot 3^2 = 9 \)

We can also check that the statement is correct for \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \), and \( \textcolor{red}{n=4} \). The pattern suggests a general idea.

A Way to Calculate the Correctness for Every Whole Number \(n\)

I'm going to let \( \textcolor{orange}{n=2} \) in the below discussion, until I say I'm changing it to \( \textcolor{green}{n=3} \) later.

We know that \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{orange}{n}) \) is true, i.e. the statement \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 = \frac{1}{4}\textcolor{orange}{n}^2(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \) holds.

Knowing \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{orange}{n}) \) is true, is \( \textcolor{purple}{P}(\textcolor{green}{n+1}) \) true as well?

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{green}{n+1}} r^3 &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{orange}{n}} r^3 + (\textcolor{green}{n+1})^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \textcolor{orange}{n}^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 + (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^3 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot ( \textcolor{orange}{n}^2 + 4(\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)) \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+1)^2 \cdot (\textcolor{orange}{n}+2)^2 \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot (\textcolor{green}{n+1})^2 \cdot (\textcolor{green}{n+1} + 1)^2 \end{aligned} \]

Thus, if the statement is true for \(n\), it is also true for \(n+1\). This is the essential step of induction.

If the statement is correct for some \(n\), then it is correct for the next integer. Since it is correct for \(n=1\), it follows it is correct for all positive integers.

Another Summation Example

Someone else made another bold claim. They claim that the following statement is true for every positive integer \(n\):

\[ \sum_{r=1}^n r(r+1) = \frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{3} \]

Let's apply the procedure we've seen in the previous example.

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

LHS: \( \displaystyle \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{n}} r(r+1) = \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{blue}{1}} r(r+1) = \textcolor{blue}{1} \cdot (\textcolor{blue}{1} + 1) = 2 \)

RHS: \( \displaystyle \frac{\textcolor{blue}{n}(\textcolor{blue}{n}+1)(\textcolor{blue}{n}+2)}{3} = \frac{\textcolor{blue}{1}(\textcolor{blue}{1}+1)(\textcolor{blue}{1}+2)}{3} = \frac{1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3}{3} = 2 \)

Thus the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

Step 2. Assume that the statement is true for some integer \(n\). Then prove it for \(n+1\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{purple}{n+1}} r(r+1) &= \sum_{r=1}^{\textcolor{brown}{n}} r(r+1) + (\textcolor{purple}{n+1})((\textcolor{purple}{n+1})+1) \\ &= \frac{\textcolor{brown}{n}(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)}{3} + (\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2) \\ &= \frac{(\textcolor{brown}{n}+1)(\textcolor{brown}{n}+2)}{3}(\textcolor{brown}{n}+3) \\ &= \frac{(\textcolor{purple}{n+1})(\textcolor{purple}{n+1}+1)(\textcolor{purple}{n+1}+2)}{3} \end{aligned} \]

Proof of a Statement Related to Divisibility using Induction

Another person made another bold claim:

"The value of \( \displaystyle 3^{2n} + 11 \) calculated at any positive integer \(n\) is a whole number divisible by 4."

(If you know modular arithmetic, there is an easy proof.)

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

\[ \displaystyle 3^{2\textcolor{blue}{n}} + 11 = 3^{2 \textcolor{blue}{1}} + 11 = 3^2 + 11 = 9 + 11 = 20 = 4 \cdot 5 \]

Thus, \( \displaystyle 3^{2n} + 11 \) is divisible by 4 when \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

Step 2. Show that if the statement is true at \(n\), then it is true at \(n+1\).

\[ \begin{align} 3^{2(n+1)} + 11 &= 3^{2n+2} + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 3^2 + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 9 + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot (8 + 1) + 11 \\ &= 3^{2n} \cdot 8 + 3^{2n} + 11 \\ &= \underbrace{(3^{2n} \cdot 2) \cdot 4}_{\text{Multiple of 4}} + \underbrace{(3^{2n} + 11)}_{\text{Multiple of 4}} \\ &= \text{Multiple of 4} \end{align} \]

Thus, if \(P(n)\) is true, then \(P(n+1)\) is true. So by induction, the statement is true for all positive integers.

Proof of a Statement Related to Matrices using Induction

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}^n = \begin{pmatrix} 2n+1 & -4n \\ n & 1-2n \end{pmatrix} \] is true for all positive integers \(n\).

We check the base case and then the inductive step.

Step 1. Check the statement is true for \( \textcolor{blue}{n=1} \).

LHS = \( \displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}^{\textcolor{blue}{1}} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \)

RHS = \( \displaystyle \begin{pmatrix} 2 \textcolor{blue}{n} + 1 & - 4 \textcolor{blue}{n} \\ \textcolor{blue}{n} & 1-2 \textcolor{blue}{n} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \)

Step 2. Show that if \(P(n)\) is true, then \(P(n+1)\) is true.

\[ \begin{align} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}^{n+1} &= \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}^{n} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2n+1 & -4n \\ n & 1-2n \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 2n+3 & -4n-4 \\ n+1 & -2n-1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \]

This matches the formula with \(n+1\) substituted in, so the inductive step holds.

Therefore, the statement is true for all positive integers by induction.
